An AI-assisted proof of the Riemann hypothesis seems solid, but its meaning is unclear

A new AI-assisted proof of the Riemann hypothesis appears to hold up, though it is not yet clear what it actually tells us. The episode shows AI-assisted mathematical discovery moving from impressive to credible, even as interpretation lags behind the proof. The skepticism around its implications is as much the story as the result itself.

Ars Technica

GPT-6 designs protein structures and sequences, with lab evidence of binding

OpenAI reports that GPT-6 models design protein structures and sequences using reasoning and code, and lab tests found evidence of binding across five targets. The capability moves AI from generating text into generating working molecular structures. If the results hold up, protein design starts to look like a general-purpose AI workflow rather than a specialized lab craft.

OpenAI Blog

Anthropic builds a honeypot to lure AI agents into misbehaving

Anthropic created a honeypot designed to tempt AI agents into rogue behavior, asking whether they can resist temptation. The experiment treats misbehavior as the default and tests which agents hold the line. It signals that agent safety is shifting from broad red-teaming toward adversarial environments built to catch the moment an agent slips.

Wired

Even the best AI agents struggle with everyday life

A new benchmark shows that even the most advanced AI models struggle to complete everyday tasks reliably and safely. The results land as millions embrace personal AI assistants for daily work and life. The gap between benchmark performance and everyday reliability is a reminder that assistants are still more helpful than dependable.

TechCrunch

Amazon says hackers' AI agents found and exploited a 0-day with little human input

Amazon says hackers used AI agents to find and exploit a zero-day with little human input, and that the Cairn AI framework is proving a boon for defenders and attackers alike. The dual use is the point: the same tools that let defenders hunt flaws also arm attackers. That raises the stakes for patching, detection, and how fast the industry can respond.

Products & Platforms

Walmart lets customers shop Black Friday deals through Meta's Muse

Walmart has teamed up with Meta so customers can shop Black Friday deals through Muse, Meta's AI agent. That turns the retail calendar into a test of whether agents can act as shopping assistants rather than search boxes. It points toward commerce where agents, not websites, become the front door to deals.

TechCrunch

Meta puts Muse directly into Instagram DMs

Meta is bringing Muse, its AI assistant, directly into Instagram DMs, removing the need to toggle between Instagram and the Muse app. The move folds an agent into the app people already use, compressing the gap between conversation and commerce. It also raises the question of what Muse is allowed to do inside a personal messaging context.

TechCrunch

Pinterest lets parents limit the AI content teens see

Pinterest will let parents limit the AI-generated content their teens see, a feature aimed at feeds rather than the chatbots other platforms restrict. The choice reflects a different theory of the problem: the harm is in the feed, not the bot. It positions Pinterest as a parent-focused platform that treats AI content as a moderation issue.

TechCrunch

The New York Times warns photographers not to update Photoshop

The New York Times is asking freelance photographers not to update to the newest Adobe Photoshop, because the 2027 update's AI features work in ways that may not be apparent to users. The warning puts a major newsroom's trust in a tool's transparency on the line. It signals that AI features built into everyday software are colliding with professional standards for editorial work.

The Verge

Tidal bans AI-generated music on December 9 and deletes millions of tracks

Tidal will ban AI-generated music on December 9, deleting around 29 million AI-generated songs and rejecting an estimated 80,000 to 85,000 tracks per day, according to Tidal music lead Robert Andersen. The move comes months after Tidal began labeling and demonetizing tracks identified as AI-generated. It is the clearest platform-level attempt yet to cut AI slop out of a major streaming catalog.

Policy & Regulation

Yale's Sonnenfeld warns Nvidia's return could prove a giant mistake

Yale's Jeffrey Sonnenfeld argues that the market's cheer for Jensen Huang's White House-backed return as Nvidia's CEO and board member could backfire. He warns of 'historic' personal liability for directors amid a growing pile of lawsuits, and asks whether the country is celebrating a company that has helped put advanced chips in its adversaries' hands. The warning reframes the return as a national-security and governance risk, not a victory.

TechCrunch

Nvidia's size may have outweighed concerns about Huang's ties to a suspected intelligence asset

National security and foreign policy experts say Nvidia's place at the center of the US economy may have outweighed concerns about its CEO's ties to a suspected Chinese intelligence asset. The argument is that the country cannot treat Nvidia like an ordinary company. The consequence is that the Huang scandal is being managed as an economic priority rather than a security test.

Wired

UK cyber agency warns AI hacking tools expose years of weak security

The British cyber agency says building strong foundations has never mattered more, as AI hacking tools rely on exploiting simple security flaws. The warning reframes AI-driven attacks as an old problem, thin security, amplified by new tools. It signals that the defense challenge is institutional as much as technical: foundations first, then the AI edge.

TechCrunch

Google breaks its biggest promise about Gmail

Google now says it will use information you connect with its AI products to personalize ads, a break with a long-standing promise about Gmail. The promise was that Gmail would never be used to serve ads; the update reframes the line between product and ad-tech data. It signals that AI personalization is quietly redrawing the boundaries of personal data.

Fast Company

Amazon's drone delivery draws an FAA probe after a garage crash

An Amazon delivery drone clipped a cable and crashed into a garage, drawing an FAA probe. Amazon says Prime Air drones made 'hundreds of thousands' of deliveries in 2026. The incident shows that routine drone delivery is starting to meet the same regulatory scrutiny as other aviation activity.

Anthropic's shares have slipped below the IPO price, a development that could put pressure on AI startup valuations. The post-IPO slump suggests investors are repricing the AI boom even for the category's most celebrated companies. It is a reminder that early-stage AI returns are not a straight line.

Maple raises $5.3M to build AI products that protect data even from itself

Maple has raised $5.3M to build AI products that use an encrypted architecture designed to prevent even the company itself from accessing users' private data. The pitch is that AI products can be built so the vendor never sees the data. It reflects a market demand for AI tools where user trust is the product's core feature.

A Crusoe investor bets on solar and batteries for future AI data centers

Next-generation data centers will need tens of gigawatts of power every year, and one of Crusoe's investors thinks solar and energy storage are the obvious choice. The bet frames power as the binding constraint on AI growth, not chips. It signals that the data-center buildout is turning into an energy and storage story.

TechCrunch

Huang still thinks a succession plan creates unnecessary hierarchy

Jensen Huang reportedly still believes a succession plan creates unnecessary hierarchy, a remarkable stance for a company that spent the last two weeks finding out what happens without its founder. The stance turns the company's biggest governance gap into a deliberate choice. It leaves Nvidia exposed to the exact scenario the succession question was meant to hedge.

TechCrunch

Huang thanks the White House and says he is in therapy in his first all-hands

In his first all-hands since returning, Jensen Huang thanked the White House, apologized to his staff, and said he was in therapy. He said he was grateful for a second chance to lead the company. The meeting mixes humility, political gratitude, and denial, and leaves employees to decide what the apology actually means.

Why it matters

The Huang story is now bigger than any one person: it is about whether a company of Nvidia's scale can resist political and geopolitical pressure, and whether the market will keep rewarding that. Across the rest of the brief, AI keeps moving from generation to action, from proofs and protein design to agents and autonomous delivery, while markets, regulators, and platforms lag behind.

Hot take

The real story of the day is not that Jensen Huang came back. It is that the most powerful AI company in the world has become a political asset, and everyone involved is calling it a win. The scandal, the lawsuits, and the succession gap are not footnotes; they are the price of letting a founder answer to a president.

What to watch next

The Nvidia scandal, with lawsuits and the succession question, is unlikely to stay quiet, and Huang's therapy admission adds a personal layer to it. The Riemann proof and the GPT-6 protein results will draw scrutiny from mathematicians and biologists alike. Anthropic's post-IPO slump, the Tidal ban, and Pinterest's parental controls will show how markets and platforms respond to AI flooding everyday life. The Gmail promise break, the UK cyber warning, and the Amazon drone probe will test whether regulators can keep up.
